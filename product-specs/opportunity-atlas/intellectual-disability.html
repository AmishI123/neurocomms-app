Comparison area · Intellectual disability

Make decisions accessible while keeping the person in control

The clearest product opportunity is accessible information and supported decision-making: people may need clearer choices, more time, communication support, and trusted help without having decisions made for them.

Atlas homeCompare areasEvidence and limits

Who this evidence represents

The evidence includes a review of supported decision-making, accessible communication research, and healthcare decision-making perspectives from supporters. Direct participation by people with intellectual disability must be increased in design and evaluation. Supporters are not interchangeable with the person whose decision is being made.

Design consequence: the person is the decision-maker. A supporter may explain, remember, or help communicate, but the system must show whose preference was expressed and who changed the record.

Sources: decision-making review, accessible communication report, and healthcare decision-making study.

Possible products to explore

Accessible choice board

Shows options in short text, pictures, audio, or symbols and allows the person to ask for more information.

Decision conversation record

Keeps the person’s questions, preferences, supporters, and final choice distinct and reviewable.

Healthcare participation preparation

Prepares questions and communication preferences without turning the tool into a caregiver-owned medical file.

Pain points and product requirements

1. Information is often inaccessible at the moment of choice

Long text, abstract language, fast speech, unfamiliar forms, and missing context can prevent a person from comparing options.

Example: A person is asked to agree to a service but receives only a long document and no chance to ask what will happen next.
Possible product: an accessible explanation view with one choice at a time, examples, audio or symbols, and a question button.
  • show the original information alongside the explanation;
  • allow “I do not know yet” and “I want someone to explain”;
  • never replace consent with a simplified summary.
Acceptance check: the user can ask a question, change a choice, and review the explanation before sharing a response.

2. Support can override preference

Family, staff, or professionals may answer because they know the person well or because the process is rushed.

Example: A supporter selects an appointment option that is convenient for them, while the person preferred another time.
Possible product: a decision record with separate fields for the person’s choice, support offered, and unresolved disagreement.
  • make the person’s selection visually primary;
  • record support without turning it into surveillance;
  • allow the person to revise the decision later.
Acceptance check: the system cannot silently replace the person’s choice with a supporter’s entry.

3. Communication needs change by setting

A person may use speech, signs, pictures, writing, AAC, or a trusted partner depending on the topic, people present, and energy available.

Example: The person speaks at home but needs symbols and extra time in a clinic with unfamiliar staff.
Possible product: a user-owned communication preference card that can be adapted for each setting.
  • support multiple modes without ranking them;
  • include a private refusal or stop message;
  • keep preferences editable by the person.
Acceptance check: the person can choose what a supporter or service sees for this interaction only.

4. Records can become tools of control

Notes about choices, behaviour, or support can be reused by institutions in ways the person did not expect.

Example: A private note about needing more time is copied into a permanent file and later used to limit opportunities.
Possible product: local, time-bounded records with clear audience, retention, export, and deletion controls.
  • make sharing opt-in and specific;
  • show who can see a record and for how long;
  • avoid behavioural scores and compliance labels.
Acceptance check: the person can delete or correct a record and see a complete sharing history.

Evidence boundary and next test

A safe first test is a fictional decision-support flow co-designed and evaluated with self-advocates. It must not make legal, medical, financial, or safeguarding decisions. A caregiver-only dashboard would fail the central product requirement.
