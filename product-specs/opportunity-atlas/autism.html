Area 1 · Autism

Products that give people more control over communication and surroundings

The initial evidence points to problems with sensory conditions, processing time, communication with services, administrative tasks, and being misunderstood. The same support will not suit every autistic person.

Atlas homeCompare areasEvidence and limits

Who this evidence represents

Autistic adults are represented in the main healthcare and communication studies used here. Other sources include online discussion analysis and service research. Autistic children, people who use AAC, people with intellectual disability, and people from non-English-speaking settings are not represented equally in this first pass.

What the evidence suggests

Autistic adults in recent studies described healthcare barriers linked to sensory discomfort, communication, processing time, and providers’ limited knowledge. Other work describes inaccessible administrative systems, inaccessible environments, stigma, and two-way misunderstandings in communication.

Design consequence: a product should help a person control the interaction and the setting. It should not guess feelings, force eye contact, or train a person to perform a non-autistic style of communication.

Sources: healthcare encounters, quality of life and ageing, nonverbal communication, sensory-overload accommodation discussion, and healthcare communication discussion.

Possible products to explore

Communication conditions card

Lets a person choose how to ask for time, written information, quieter surroundings, or a different way to communicate.

Healthcare visit preparation

Helps a person prepare questions, preferred communication methods, and private notes before an appointment.

Service navigation guide

Breaks a referral, benefits, or support process into clear steps and shows what information is still unknown.

Pain points and product requirements

1. The surroundings make communication harder

Noise, bright lights, crowds, waiting, and unpredictable changes can use up attention before a conversation begins.

Example: A person knows what they want to tell a clinician, but the waiting room is loud and the appointment moves quickly. By the time they are called in, they cannot organise the information.
Possible product: a private “conditions for communication” card. The person chooses or writes: “please give me a minute,” “written information helps,” “reduce background noise,” or “ask one question at a time.”

Must do:

  • let the person choose the wording and the audience;
  • allow a card to be shown, printed, or copied without sharing a diagnosis;
  • include “this is not useful right now” and “I need to stop” options.
Acceptance check: a person can create and edit a request without entering a diagnosis, mood, or reason they do not want to disclose.

2. People need more processing time

A spoken question may disappear before the person has understood it, decided what they think, and prepared an answer.

Example: A doctor asks three questions quickly. The person answers the last one, forgets the first two, and later remembers important information they wanted to share.
Possible product: appointment preparation with a question list, written answer space, and a reviewable visit summary.

Must do:

  • let the person prepare in writing, symbols, speech, or another method;
  • show unanswered questions without saying they were forgotten or failed;
  • keep the summary editable and private until the person chooses to share it.
Acceptance check: the user can prepare, reorder, remove, and export questions. The tool never sends a summary automatically.

3. Administrative systems are hard to use

Forms, phone calls, referrals, benefits, and service websites can require many steps and can assume fast spoken communication.

Example: A person knows which service they need but cannot complete the phone call, remember the required documents, and track the follow-up at the same time.
Possible product: a service-navigation page that breaks one process into “what this service does,” “what they may ask for,” “what I need to prepare,” and “what happens next.”

Must do:

  • show unknown information as unknown instead of filling gaps with guesses;
  • offer written, phone, and in-person routes when the source lists them;
  • save only the follow-up notes the user chooses to keep.
Acceptance check: every service claim has a source and date checked. The user can mark a step as not applicable.

4. Misunderstanding is treated as one person’s fault

Communication can fail because both people use different signals, assumptions, timing, and meanings. A tool that assumes the autistic person is the only problem can add harm.

Example: A manager reads a short message as rude. The employee meant it as a clear answer because they were concentrating on the work.
Possible product: a mutual communication agreement with options such as direct wording, written decisions, extra response time, and a way to check meaning.

Must do:

  • describe communication preferences as choices, not rules about how the person must behave;
  • let both parties edit the agreement;
  • avoid interpreting facial expression, gaze, tone, or silence as proof of intent.
Acceptance check: the tool never labels a communication style as dishonest, rude, compliant, or socially appropriate.

5. A transition can remove the information needed to feel prepared

Moving between rooms, tasks, services, or life stages can involve new people, rules, sensory conditions, and uncertainty.

Example: A person can attend a familiar appointment but has difficulty when the clinic changes the entrance, waiting area, or order of events.
Possible product: a user-authored transition plan with a map or photo if wanted, expected steps, changes to watch for, and a choice to pause.

Must do:

  • show what is confirmed and what may change;
  • allow the person to remove details that make the plan worse;
  • keep the ability to communicate “I do not want to continue.”
Acceptance check: the user can create two versions: a short version for another person and a private full version for themselves.

Non-goals and open questions

  • Do not infer overload, anxiety, pain, consent, or preference from behaviour.
  • Do not build a compliance tracker or a tool that rewards looking calm.
  • Do not assume every autistic person wants schedules, social scripts, or sensory tools.
  • Ask autistic people which situations matter, what language feels respectful, and what control they need before testing a product.

The current page is an initial product hypothesis based on the sources above. It needs broader first-person and community evidence before a priority is chosen.
