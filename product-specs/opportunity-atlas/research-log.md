# Research log

## 15 September 2026: first source pass

### Search purpose

Find recent evidence about practical problems and possible support products for autism, Down syndrome, dyslexia, AAC, and five comparison areas.

### Main search lanes used

- autism autistic adults communication sensory environment transition barriers qualitative study;
- Down syndrome speech language communication literacy access qualitative study;
- dyslexia adults reading workplace assistive technology qualitative study;
- AAC users communication device access privacy vocabulary partner barriers systematic review;
- developmental language disorder adult participation;
- dyscalculia adult money time daily life;
- developmental coordination disorder adult participation and daily tasks;
- intellectual disability accessible information and decision making;
- aphasia communication access and social participation.

### Source decisions

- Kept peer-reviewed studies, PubMed records, PMC full text, official guidance, and a small number of clearly labelled community posts.
- Kept a conference abstract book as an aphasia lead only; it does not support a finished aphasia product page.
- Kept older dyslexia read-aloud evidence because it is a useful outcome review, while labelling its age and limits.
- Kept provider and advocacy guidance separate from independent research.
- Did not convert search-result counts into prevalence.

### Evidence patterns found

Autism: sensory environment, processing time, healthcare communication, administration, and mutual misunderstanding.

Down syndrome: speech intelligibility can hide language ability; AAC and literacy support can help but adult evidence is thinner; partner behaviour affects participation.

Dyslexia: reading and writing effort, document format, fatigue, workplace support, and limited adult assistive-technology evaluation.

AAC: device access, privacy, partner knowledge, flexible input and output, vocabulary, funding, repair, and the right to create new messages.

Comparison: DLD points to adult participation; dyscalculia to money, time, and quantity; DCD to motor and daily-task access; intellectual disability to accessible information and supported decision-making; aphasia to a separate acquired-communication lane.
