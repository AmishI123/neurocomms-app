Area 3 · Dyslexia

Products that reduce the cost of reading and writing tasks

The initial evidence points to slow or tiring reading, missed words, comprehension problems in dense material, writing and spelling effort, hard-to-use documents, and uncertainty about workplace support.

Who this evidence represents

The evidence includes adult learners, workplace guidance, research participants, and public community discussions. The first pass has more evidence about reading support than about specific workplace software, cultural differences, or people who do not use the dyslexia label.

What the evidence suggests

A 2024 review found that assistive-technology research for adults with dyslexia is scarce, especially in work and higher education. Research on read-aloud tools suggests possible comprehension benefits with substantial variation. Community accounts describe fatigue, line tracking, skipped words, dense documents, and the practical cost of writing and checking.

Design consequence: support should be chosen for the actual task. The product must not assume that a special font, colour, voice, or reading mode works for every dyslexic person.

Sources: adult assistive-technology review, read-aloud meta-analysis, adult reading enjoyment study, public reading discussion, and professional workplace narratives.

Possible products to explore

Document access workspace

Helps a person read dense, scanned, or badly formatted documents while keeping the original visible.

Reading and writing support

Combines read-aloud, line focus, speech-to-text, draft read-back, and editable checking for a specific task.

Private adjustment request builder

Creates a request for extra time, information in advance, digital text, or another support without requiring medical details.

Pain points and product requirements

1. Reading can take too much time and energy

A person may read accurately but slowly, lose their place, reread, skip small words, or need breaks before the meaning is clear.

Example: An employee understands the subject but needs an hour to read a short policy. By the end, they are too tired to write the response it requires.
Possible product: a reading workspace with user-controlled read-aloud, line focus, spacing, speed, and a place to write a short understanding check.

Must do:

  • let the person change one support at a time;
  • keep the original text available for checking;
  • avoid changing wording without showing exactly what changed.
Acceptance check: the user can read, pause, replay, change speed, and return to the same line without losing the source document.

2. Important documents are badly formatted or scanned

PDFs, photographs, forms, columns, tables, and small text can make ordinary reading tools fail.

Example: A person can use read-aloud in an email but receives a scanned PDF for a workplace task. The text cannot be selected, the columns are read in the wrong order, and the person cannot tell which number belongs to which field.
Possible product: a document-access layer that detects selectable text, lets the user check extracted text, and keeps the page image beside the reading view.

Must do:

  • show when text was extracted or may be wrong;
  • preserve tables, headings, and page references where possible;
  • never silently rewrite a document that could change its meaning.
Acceptance check: the user can compare the extracted sentence with the original page before relying on it.

3. Writing shows a different set of barriers

Putting ideas into words, spelling, organising a message, checking missing words, and typing can each require attention.

Example: Someone knows the answer to an email but leaves out small words, mixes sentence order, and cannot notice the errors until they read it several times.
Possible product: a writing workspace with speech-to-text, a visible draft structure, read-back, and user-controlled checking rather than automatic rewriting.

Must do:

  • separate the person’s wording from optional suggestions;
  • highlight possible errors without changing the draft automatically;
  • let the person hear the draft and compare it with what they meant.
Acceptance check: the original draft remains recoverable after any suggested correction.

4. Support at school or work can require disclosure

A person may need extra time, information in advance, a quiet place, or assistive technology but worry about being judged or having to explain private information.

Example: A student needs text-to-speech for an exam but is told that asking for it means proving they cannot read.
Possible product: a private adjustment-request builder organised around the task and the change needed.

Must do:

  • start with “what would make this task accessible?”;
  • make diagnosis and personal history optional;
  • show a complete editable request before copying or sending.
Acceptance check: a request for digital text and extra time can be produced without entering a diagnosis.

5. Existing tools may be hard to compare and learn

A person may be offered several reading or writing tools without knowing which one matches the task, costs money, works with their files, or needs training.

Example: Someone has access to a text-to-speech app but cannot use it on scanned documents and does not know whether another tool would help before paying for it.
Possible product: a task-based tool comparison guide that tests a small sample document and reports what each tool can and cannot do.

Must do:

  • compare real tasks such as scanned PDF, email, form, and handwritten notes;
  • show cost, device, privacy, and training requirements;
  • avoid ranking a tool without user-relevant evidence.
Acceptance check: every comparison claim has a source or a repeatable local test.

Non-goals and open questions

  • Do not promise to cure dyslexia or make reading equally easy for everyone.
  • Do not assume all reading difficulty is dyslexia or that every dyslexic person needs read-aloud.
  • Do not hide errors created by text extraction, summarising, spelling correction, or AI rewriting.
  • Study adult work and higher education tasks directly; the current evidence is much stronger for general support than for a specific adult product.

The public posts are useful problem signals but are not a prevalence estimate or a test of any tool.
