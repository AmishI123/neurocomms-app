3 / 17

Autism

Explain what helps before a conversation gets difficult

  • The setting can use up attention
  • Questions can arrive too quickly
  • Service steps can be hard to follow
  • People can misread each other
Example

An adult arrives at a clinic with three questions. The waiting room is loud. They want written questions and a quieter place, but explaining that is already difficult.

Proposed product: My conversation card. Full brief · Findings and sources

Speaker notes

The saved evidence includes adult healthcare studies and public discussions. It gives less coverage to children, people who use communication aids, and people who cannot take part in text-based research. Explain the proposal through the example. Ask: Will people choose to show a card, and will the recipient make the requested change?

4 / 17

Down syndrome

Help a person say more than someone else expects

  • Unfamiliar people may miss the message
  • Speech does not show everything a person knows
  • Support affects who gets heard
  • Adult learning needs more attention
Example

At a café, a person wants to change an order. Their usual supporter understands them, but the cashier does not. They need a way to show the change and confirm it themselves.

Proposed product: My words for today. Full brief · Findings and sources

Speaker notes

Some sources describe children, professionals, or family views. The adult AAC literacy study involved three adults and reported variable modest gains. It does not establish that the product proposed here teaches reading. Explain the proposal through the example. Ask: Which text, images, or other methods does each person actually want to use?

5 / 17

Dyslexia

Read a difficult document and check your own words

  • Reading can take extra time and effort
  • A readable file is not always available
  • Writing involves repeated checking
  • Help depends on the workplace or course
Example

A student must find three requirements in a long course handout and write an email about one unclear point. They repeatedly lose their place and worry about the email wording.

Proposed product: Reading and writing desk. Full brief · Findings and sources

Speaker notes

The saved source mix includes reviews, workplace accounts, and public posts. Tool preferences vary. The report does not establish one best font, colour, voice, or reading method. Explain the proposal through the example. Ask: Does keeping reading and writing together help more than tools people already use?

6 / 17

Communication aids (AAC)

Keep a useful way to communicate when the usual tool is unavailable

  • The device may be out of reach
  • A fixed list can limit the message
  • Conversation partners matter
  • Private messages need protection
Example

A person’s usual device is charging during an outing. They need a few chosen messages and a way to tell a listener that none of those messages is what they mean.

Proposed product: My communication backup. Full brief · Findings and sources

Speaker notes

The saved evidence mixes user accounts, research, and views from supporters and professionals. Device access differs widely. A generic backup cannot promise the same communication as a person’s usual system. Explain the proposal through the example. Ask: What important messages or access methods are lost when moving to the backup?

7 / 17

Developmental language disorder (DLD)

Work through a message and prepare your own answer

  • Fluent speech can hide difficulty
  • An answer may take time to form
  • Services require several language tasks
  • Help may become someone else’s answer
Example

A person receives a housing appointment letter. They can read parts of it but cannot work out what to bring or how to ask about a missing detail.

Proposed product: One message at a time. Full brief · Findings and sources

Speaker notes

The saved adult and young-adult studies support further investigation. Source coverage is small; examples here are invented and proposed features have not been tested. Explain the proposal through the example. Ask: Does the tool reduce language work or add another layer to read?

8 / 17

Dyscalculia

See how an everyday number was worked out

  • Time calculations affect the day
  • Money tasks can cause stress
  • Units can add confusion
  • Past criticism can make checking harder
Example

An appointment starts at 10:00. Travel takes 30 minutes and preparation takes 20. The person wants to see when to start and how that changes if travel takes longer.

Proposed product: Show me the time steps. Full brief · Findings and sources

Speaker notes

The saved daily-number study compared 26 adults with dyscalculia with 26 controls. It supports a focused task question, not a claim about every adult. Community reports are self-selected. Explain the proposal through the example. Ask: Does the visible breakdown help more than an ordinary calculator and a written example?

9 / 17

Developmental coordination disorder (DCD)

Prepare the tools and space for a task

  • Understanding a task may not make it easy to do
  • Effort can build up across a day
  • Small controls create extra work
  • Others may miss the physical difficulty
Example

A student knows what to write but loses time gathering materials, arranging the desk, and switching between handwritten notes and a laptop.

Proposed product: My task setup. Full brief · Findings and sources

Speaker notes

The saved evidence includes young-adult participation and higher-education research. It does not establish that a task app improves motor skills or that all motor difficulties have the same cause. Explain the proposal through the example. Ask: Which part is a remembering problem, and which part needs a change in tools or surroundings?

10 / 17

Intellectual disability

Make choices understandable and keep the person’s answer clear

  • Information may arrive in an unsuitable form
  • Support can change who decides
  • People use different ways to communicate
  • Records can outlast the intended conversation
Example

A person is choosing a leisure activity. A supporter prefers the nearest option, but the person wants to ask about transport before deciding.

Proposed product: My choices and questions. Full brief · Findings and sources

Speaker notes

The saved sources include a literature review, accessible-information work, and supporter perspectives. Direct review by people with intellectual disability is still needed for the proposed screens. Explain the proposal through the example. Ask: Can the person express disagreement with both the suggested options and the helper?

11 / 17

Aphasia

Keep a person involved in a healthcare conversation

  • Healthcare conversations can move too fast
  • A missing word can hide a clear idea
  • The listener affects success
  • Support can be lost between services
Example

A person wants to ask about a follow-up visit but cannot find the words quickly. The clinician starts speaking to their companion instead.

Proposed product: My visit conversation. Full brief · Findings and sources

Speaker notes

The saved sources include people with aphasia, significant others, and healthcare studies. The product proposal has not been evaluated and makes no claim to restore language. Explain the proposal through the example. Ask: Does the tool help the person take part when the clinician has limited time?

12 / 17

Several products can share basic parts

  • Write and edit personal content.
  • Preview exactly what will be shown.
  • Keep a place or an unanswered question.
  • Print or download selected content.
  • Change or remove saved work.
Speaker notes

Shared code can reduce effort, but user needs still need separate review. A layout that works for typing may fail for another access method.

The first choice has an existing starting point

Review the existing conversation-card app first. Compare a reading desk and a time-step helper as the next distinct tasks.

Choose based on the person’s task and current method, the effort added, and the control they retain.

Speaker notes

This is a recommendation from the project’s current state, not proof of superior usefulness. If existing notes or paper work as well, change direction.

14 / 17

A product brief must be specific enough to build

  • Who uses it and for which task.
  • Four screens and the choices on each.
  • Required features and exact checks.
  • Saved information and sharing rules.
  • What happens when something fails.
  • Build order and unanswered questions.
Speaker notes

Open one linked brief if time permits. The time helper includes a worked arithmetic check. The boards include correction and selective export checks.

15 / 17

What evidence would justify continuing?

  • The intended task works.
  • The person can correct a mistake and choose what to share.
  • The tool helps more than the existing method.
  • The added preparation and maintenance feel worthwhile.
  • Results state who took part and what remains unknown.
Speaker notes

Engineering checks can establish function. Accessible user reviews are needed to learn usefulness. Do not call a working prototype a proven intervention.
