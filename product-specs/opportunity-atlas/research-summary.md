# Initial research summary

## Status

This is the first evidence pass for the communication and learning product opportunity atlas. It is a starting record, not the finished HTML atlas.

The first pass found four strong, connected product areas:

1. **Autism:** people need control over communication conditions, sensory surroundings, processing time, healthcare interactions, and service systems.
2. **Down syndrome:** speech clarity and language differences can hide what a person understands or wants to say. Communication support depends on time, partner behaviour, AAC access, literacy support, and continuity across school and adult life.
3. **Dyslexia:** reading and writing can consume unusual amounts of time and energy, especially in long, badly formatted, scanned, or high-stakes documents. Adult work and higher education are less studied than children’s contexts.
4. **AAC:** a device is only one part of communication. Access, vocabulary, privacy, partner knowledge, repair, funding, and the right to say something new all shape whether it works.

## What the first pass changes about the product direction

The product should be organised around a specific barrier rather than around a diagnosis. A person might need:

- a way to ask for a quieter or more predictable interaction;
- a way to prepare for a healthcare conversation and keep control of the information shared;
- a way to read or write a difficult document with selectable support;
- a way to keep an AAC system available, private, flexible, and usable with different partners;
- a way for communication partners to wait, listen, and confirm meaning without taking over.

The strongest cross-area product opportunity is **communication access under changing conditions**. The same interface cannot be assumed to fit every person, but the product questions repeat: What is the person trying to communicate? What makes access hard right now? What control does the person have? What information is private? What happens when the main tool fails?

## Area notes

### Autism

The initial sources point to sensory environment, communication timing, healthcare access, administrative burden, and two-way misunderstanding. A useful product would let the person choose written, spoken, or supported communication; prepare a request; and record the conditions they want without inferring their feelings.

The page must avoid presenting autistic people as needing to learn non-autistic behaviour. It should treat misunderstandings as interactions involving both people and the environment.

### Down syndrome

The initial evidence points to a gap between speech intelligibility and actual language ability, communication-partner behaviour, AAC and literacy support, and a shortage of adult intervention research. A useful product may help with participation, health communication, or partner support, but it must not make family or staff the only users who matter.

The page should separate evidence from caregivers, teachers, and people with Down syndrome. It should also distinguish child communication intervention from adult participation and independence. A public discussion adds a practical distinction: a person may be understood by family but not by unfamiliar people, so a product must support communication outside the family without assuming speech practice is the only answer.

### Dyslexia

The initial evidence points to reading effort, fatigue, complex documents, writing and spelling, workplace support, and limited adult assistive-technology research. A useful product may provide task-specific document access: read aloud, follow the current line, inspect a scanned page, dictate a draft, or compare a draft with the original.

The page must avoid claiming that one font, colour, overlay, or text-to-speech voice works for all dyslexic people. The product should let the person choose and measure the task outcome, such as understanding the document or completing the form.

### AAC

The initial evidence points to communication access, privacy, partner knowledge, device fit, vocabulary, and continued use. A useful product may help preserve a person’s access and control across settings, but it must support existing AAC systems and never replace the user’s own communication method.

The page should distinguish a speech-generating device from AAC as a wider group of methods. It should include paper, signs, gestures, typing, symbols, and partner-assisted methods where relevant. Public AAC discussions add concrete conditions that formal studies may miss: rain, device reachability, mobility aids, queues, people who do not wait, and the effort of switching between speech and AAC. They also show that people may use different apps, devices, and paper systems for different situations.

## Candidate products to compare

| Product idea | Main problem | First safe version |
|---|---|---|
| Communication access card | A person cannot quickly explain the conditions or time they need | Private, editable cards for “please wait,” “written information helps,” “I need a quieter place,” and user-written alternatives |
| Healthcare conversation preparation | A person loses control during a fast or inaccessible appointment | Questions, preferred communication method, private notes, and a reviewable visit summary |
| Document access workspace | A document is too dense, scanned, fast, or tiring to read | User-controlled text extraction, read-aloud, line focus, spacing, and a way to check the original |
| AAC continuity and backup | The device, vocabulary, battery, repair, or setting fails | Exportable vocabulary and phrases, printable backup, device-independent personal messages, and private recovery notes |
| Partner waiting and confirmation tool | Other people rush, guess, or speak for the person | Short partner guidance chosen by the user, with a confirmation step and no compliance score |
| Support and funding navigator | The person or family cannot tell who provides what help | Sourced service steps, questions to ask, cost and eligibility fields, and a private follow-up record |

These are product hypotheses. The final pages must say which are supported by evidence, which are simply promising designs, and which need a specialist partner before building.

## Gaps still requiring the next pass

- Direct first-person evidence is uneven across the four areas.
- The Down syndrome search needs more adult and self-advocate sources.
- AAC evidence needs better separation between device users, caregivers, clinicians, and educators.
- Dyslexia evidence needs more real workplace and higher-education tasks rather than general reading advice.
- Autism sources need careful handling of autistic people who use AAC, people with high support needs, and people whose communication is not captured by online text.
- Community search results must be reviewed manually before any theme count is reported.
- A product page must not imply that an evidence gap is a market gap or that a technical feature is a proven intervention.
