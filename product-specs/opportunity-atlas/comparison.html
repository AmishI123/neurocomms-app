Compare the main opportunities

Area Main barrier to investigate Possible first product Special care needed
Autism Sensory conditions, communication timing, service systems, and mutual misunderstanding. Private communication-condition and appointment-preparation tool. Do not infer feelings or train compliance. Include autistic people directly.
Down syndrome Speech intelligibility, language access, literacy, partner behaviour, and support continuity. Person-controlled participation and communication support. Do not let caregivers become the only user. Separate child and adult evidence.
Dyslexia Reading and writing effort, document access, comprehension, fatigue, and disclosure. Task-based reading and writing workspace. Do not promise one font, colour, or voice works for everyone. Preserve original text.
AAC Access, vocabulary, privacy, partner support, device failure, and funding. Communication continuity and user-owned backup. Support existing systems. Communication users must control the message and research process.

Where the areas overlap

Time to respond

Autistic people, people with Down syndrome, and AAC users may need more time. A product should make waiting normal and visible. Dyslexia may also require more time for reading and composing.

More than one way to communicate

Speech, writing, symbols, signs, typing, text-to-speech, and gestures can serve different situations. A product should let the person choose instead of treating one method as the real one.

Support depends on other people

A device or app cannot solve a problem if a clinician, teacher, employer, or stranger will not wait, listen, provide accessible information, or allow the tool.

Privacy and control

Communication may include health, feelings, relationships, and identity. The user needs control over what is stored and who sees it.

Which first build is safest to test?

A small first prototype should be useful across situations while keeping the person’s control clear. The best candidates for a first engineering test are:

  1. Communication access cards: low technical risk and easy to keep private, but the wording needs direct user review.
  2. Document access workspace: clear dyslexia use case, but text extraction can introduce errors and needs careful checking.
  3. AAC backup and continuity: meaningful access problem, but exporting or handling vocabulary requires strong privacy and specialist review.
  4. Appointment preparation: can support autism, Down syndrome, AAC users, and others, but it must not become a clinical record or speak for the person.
Selection rule: choose the product with a specific user, situation, and measurable access problem. Do not choose a diagnosis because it appears to offer a bigger market.

Additional areas to scan

The detailed comparison notes and their sources are in comparison-scan.md and comparison-sources.json.

Area Why it belongs in the comparison What to decide
Developmental language disorder Language understanding and expression can be missed when speech sounds fluent. Does it add a distinct product problem beyond the communication-access work?
Dyscalculia Money, time, forms, measurement, and numbers affect daily independence. Is there a clear practical tool need beyond generic calculator features?
Dyspraxia Motor planning, handwriting, device access, and travel may block an action someone understands. Does the problem need hardware, occupational expertise, or a software layer?
Intellectual disability Accessible information, decision support, healthcare, consent, and participation matter across services. Can the product include the person instead of designing only for caregivers?
Aphasia Acquired communication loss has different causes, recovery questions, and adult service needs. Should it be a separate product area rather than merged with developmental conditions?
