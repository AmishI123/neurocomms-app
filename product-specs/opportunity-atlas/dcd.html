Comparison area · Developmental coordination disorder

Support the physical conditions of an action, not only the task list

Developmental coordination disorder (DCD), also called dyspraxia in some communities, can make movement, sequencing, handwriting, navigation, self-care, and everyday tasks effortful even when the person understands the goal.

Atlas homeCompare areasEvidence and limits

Who this evidence represents

The current pass includes young-adult participation research and higher-education qualitative work, with much more research in children than adults. DCD frequently overlaps with other conditions, and motor access varies widely. A product needs occupational-therapy input before making claims about intervention.

Design consequence: ask what physical setup, timing, tools, and fatigue conditions affect the action. Do not treat a missed action as a planning failure.

Sources: young-adult occupational participation study, higher-education qualitative study, and activity and participation measurement review.

Possible products to explore

Task setup planner

Records the physical environment, tools, order, and recovery time that make a task workable.

Accessible writing and input layer

Offers alternatives to handwriting, rapid clicking, or precise motor actions while preserving the original work.

Participation preparation tool

Helps someone describe what support they need for travel, study, work, shopping, or healthcare.

Pain points and product requirements

1. Knowing the task does not guarantee physical access

A person may understand cooking, dressing, writing, or travel instructions but struggle to coordinate the movements or sequence in real time.

Example: A student understands an assignment but loses time setting up materials, managing handwriting, and switching between tools.
Possible product: a user-authored setup plan that records tools, position, order, and a stopping point.
  • allow photos, short text, voice, and checkboxes;
  • separate preparation from the task itself;
  • include a recovery or pause step.
Acceptance check: the user can change the physical setup without being shown a productivity score or completion penalty.

2. Motor effort and fatigue accumulate

Small actions, awkward interfaces, travel, and repeated corrections can consume capacity before the important part of the activity begins.

Example: A person can attend class but has little energy left after carrying materials, navigating a crowded route, and taking handwritten notes.
Possible product: an energy-aware participation plan with alternatives such as typed notes, rest, remote attendance, or a shorter task.
  • make lower-demand routes first-class options;
  • never assume slower means less committed;
  • avoid alarms that create pressure.
Acceptance check: the user can pause or choose an alternative without losing the saved plan.

3. Interfaces add unnecessary precision demands

Small targets, drag-and-drop, rapid timers, handwriting, and complex forms can make digital access harder than the underlying task.

Example: A form is readable, but the person cannot reliably select tiny controls or correct a text field before it times out.
Possible product: a forgiving input layer with large targets, keyboard alternatives, undo, and slower interaction modes.
  • support keyboard, switch, voice, and touch where feasible;
  • make every destructive action reversible;
  • do not infer intent from a missed click.
Acceptance check: the full flow works without a timed gesture or precision drag.

4. Others see the outcome but not the hidden work

Late work, spills, slow writing, or avoidance can be interpreted as carelessness rather than a mismatch between the environment and the person.

Example: A manager sees a late form but does not see that the employee needed an alternative input method and extra setup time.
Possible product: a private accommodation request that describes conditions and options without requiring a diagnosis.
  • let the user choose what to disclose;
  • describe support needs rather than deficits;
  • keep employer or school access opt-in.
Acceptance check: the user can produce a request without exposing private health information.

Evidence boundary and next test

A safe first test is a task-setup and accommodation-preparation tool, not a motor-training intervention. Review it with an occupational therapist and adults with DCD before making claims about improved performance.
