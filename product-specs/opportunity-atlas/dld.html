Comparison area · Developmental language disorder

Make information understandable without taking the person’s voice away

Developmental language disorder (DLD) can affect understanding and using language across school, work, services, and relationships. Fluent-sounding speech does not prove that information was understood.

Who this evidence represents

The current pass combines adult lived-experience research, participatory work with young adults, and service-oriented evidence. Adults are still underrepresented compared with children. People who use AAC, people with co-occurring learning differences, and people communicating in languages other than English need direct inclusion in the next pass.

Design consequence: support comprehension and expression in the moment. Do not infer that a person lacks intelligence, effort, or decision-making ability because language is difficult.

Sources: adult lived-experience study and participatory independent-living study.

Possible products to explore

Understandable service steps

Turns a form, appointment, or process into short, checkable steps while preserving the original information.

Personal response workspace

Lets a person prepare an answer using words, pictures, speech, typing, or a trusted communication partner.

Decision conversation aid

Shows options, questions, and consequences in an editable format without deciding for the person.

Pain points and product requirements

1. Fluent speech can hide a language access problem

A person may speak smoothly while missing implied meaning, long instructions, unfamiliar vocabulary, or the order of several steps.

Example: A service worker explains three documents needed for an application. The person agrees in the moment, then cannot remember which document comes first.
Possible product: a plain-language checklist that keeps the official wording available and lets the user confirm each step.
  • show one idea at a time and allow replay or rereading;
  • let the person mark “I understand,” “I need another explanation,” or “I disagree”;
  • never treat a yes/no answer as proof of comprehension.
Acceptance check: the user can reorder, edit, or reject an explanation and can see the original source before sharing a response.

2. Finding words and building an answer takes time

The person may know what they mean but need extra time, choices, keywords, or another communication mode to express it.

Example: At a healthcare visit, the person remembers an important symptom but cannot produce the word before the clinician changes topic.
Possible product: a preparation board with personal words, images, short prompts, and a way to add an unexpected answer.
  • support typing, drawing, symbols, speech, and partner-assisted entry;
  • make silence and revision normal;
  • keep the person as the author of the final message.
Acceptance check: a user can create a message without choosing from a closed list and can remove partner-entered text before export.

3. Services assume independent language navigation

Forms, appointments, housing, education, and employment processes often require interpreting dense instructions and explaining a problem in the expected format.

Example: A person misses a deadline because the important requirement is buried in a long letter and no one explained what action was needed.
Possible product: a sourced process view with “what this means,” “what I choose,” “what is still unknown,” and “who can help.”
  • keep dates, conditions, and uncertainty visible;
  • avoid rewriting legal or medical claims as if they were advice;
  • let the person ask a supporter for help without granting automatic control.
Acceptance check: every simplified step links to its source, and the user can export questions rather than being pushed to a decision.

4. Support can become substitution

A helper may answer quickly, guess the person’s meaning, or make a choice because the conversation is taking too long.

Example: A family member answers a benefits question for the person, but the answer is not what the person wanted recorded.
Possible product: a supported-decision view that records the person’s words separately from suggestions or explanations from others.
  • label who added each note;
  • offer private review before sharing;
  • include “I want to answer myself” and “I need more time.”
Acceptance check: the exported record distinguishes the person’s choice from support provided by another person.

Evidence boundary and next test

The strongest safe first test is a user-controlled service or healthcare conversation preparation tool. Measure whether a person can find the next step, express a correction, and review what will be shared. Do not claim improved language ability or clinical benefit from a prototype.

More evidence is needed from adults, self-advocates, AAC users, multilingual speakers, and people with different support needs.
