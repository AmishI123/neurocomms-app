Area 4 · AAC

Products that protect a person’s access to communication

Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) includes ways to communicate when speech alone is not enough: signs, gestures, writing, picture or symbol systems, communication books, apps, and speech-generating devices.

Who this evidence represents

The evidence includes AAC users, autistic adults, families, educators, clinicians, and research reviews. These groups are not interchangeable. The first pass needs more direct participation from AAC users with different motor, vision, hearing, literacy, language, and access needs.

What the evidence suggests

AAC research describes frustration when people cannot express themselves, loss of privacy, device and service barriers, and the importance of communication partners. Recent studies also point to input and output flexibility, device fit, vocabulary, continued use, and control of communication.

Design consequence: the product must support the person’s existing AAC system and right to say something new. A device is not the same thing as a person’s voice, and a caregiver’s report is not automatically the person’s message.

Sources: AAC user evidence synthesis, AAC research methods review, AAC barrier study, AAC application interviews, AAC user access discussion, and device and app discussion.

Possible products to explore

AAC continuity and backup

Helps a person keep selected messages, a printable backup, and recovery information available when a device fails.

Novel message builder

Supports new, private, and complex messages instead of limiting communication to a fixed list of requests.

Partner support guide

Teaches chosen communication partners to wait, listen, model, and confirm without taking over.

Repair and funding navigator

Keeps private notes about device contacts, repair steps, training, funding questions, and a backup plan.

Pain points and product requirements

1. The device may not be available when communication matters

A device can be out of reach, out of battery, broken, left in another room, not allowed in a setting, or difficult to access under time pressure.

Example: A person arrives at an appointment and the device is in a bag with a dead battery. They have a paper backup, but it contains only basic requests and not the private question they need to ask.
Possible product: an AAC continuity and backup tool that helps the person keep a private export, printable backup, and device-independent personal messages.

Must do:

  • let the user choose what is exported and who can see it;
  • support paper, typing, signs, symbols, and another device as possible backups;
  • show when a backup is old without calling it a failure.
Acceptance check: the user can create a small backup from their own chosen messages and delete it without changing the main AAC system.

2. Finding a word or phrase can take too long

A person may know what they want to say, but the device may require too many screens, an unfamiliar layout, or a search method that does not match how they think.

Example: Someone wants to tell a friend about a new event. The device has food and school folders but no way to quickly create a new sentence about the event.
Possible product: a flexible message builder with typing, symbols, stored phrases, spelling support, and a clear way to create novel messages.

Must do:

  • support more than fixed buttons and basic requests;
  • keep user-created phrases visible and exportable;
  • never silently replace the user’s wording with an AI-generated sentence.
Acceptance check: a user can make a new message, inspect the exact text, edit it, and choose whether to speak or show it.

3. Other people may take over the conversation

Partners may answer for the person, move too fast, guess what they mean, or treat device use as less real than speech.

Example: A waiter asks the person a question, then looks to the caregiver after the first pause. The person’s answer is never heard.
Possible product: a user-controlled partner guide with short instructions: talk to me, wait, do not finish for me, and check what you heard.

Must do:

  • let the user choose the instructions and audience;
  • include a confirmation step rather than a demand for fast answers;
  • avoid recording partner behaviour or scoring whether they complied.
Acceptance check: partner guidance can be shown without revealing the user’s diagnosis, history, or private messages.

4. Privacy and personal expression may be missing

A system may make public requests easy while making private, emotional, humorous, intimate, or identity-related communication difficult.

Example: A person can say “I want water” in a public setting but cannot quickly say something private to one friend without others seeing the screen.
Possible product: user-controlled private channels, message visibility choices, and a personal phrase area that is not shared with caregivers by default.

Must do:

  • make the audience visible before a message is shown or spoken;
  • allow deletion and private use;
  • never treat a private message as suspicious because it is private.
Acceptance check: the user can mark a message private, choose a recipient, and remove the message from the device history.

5. The people around the user may not know how to support AAC

Families, teachers, clinicians, peers, and healthcare workers may have different levels of knowledge. A device can be present without being meaningfully available.

Example: A school has the device but staff do not model it, do not know how to add words, or use it only when they want the student to answer.
Possible product: a short partner-learning tool chosen by the AAC user, with examples of waiting, modelling, confirming, and respecting refusal.

Must do:

  • teach partners to support communication, not to test or reward compliance;
  • let the AAC user remove guidance that feels wrong;
  • show uncertainty and ask the partner to confirm meaning.
Acceptance check: a partner can learn the chosen support steps without gaining access to private vocabulary or usage logs.

6. Access to devices, repair, training, and funding is fragmented

Getting an AAC system may involve assessment, funding, a supplier, training, repairs, updates, and several people who do not share information.

Example: A family receives a device but does not know who to call when the mount breaks, how to replace a battery, or how to get a new word added.
Possible product: a private support and repair navigator with contact details, questions to ask, device information, and a log of requests.

Must do:

  • show which information is confirmed, local, or out of date;
  • keep device serial numbers and health information out unless needed and protected;
  • include a backup communication plan while waiting for repair.
Acceptance check: a user can record a repair request and the next follow-up without exposing the record to a vendor or school by default.

Non-goals and open questions

  • Do not claim an app gives someone a voice unless the user controls the message.
  • Do not replace an AAC assessment, speech-language support, occupational support, or device funding process.
  • Do not measure success by number of taps, words spoken, compliance, or time spent on the device.
  • Include AAC users directly in research and design, with communication access and enough response time.
