Comparison area · Dyscalculia

Make everyday numbers checkable, visible, and less threatening

Adult dyscalculia evidence points beyond school arithmetic: money, time, measurement, quantity, codes, schedules, and the shame of having to trust someone else’s calculation.

Who this evidence represents

The evidence includes a small adult daily-life study, a 2025 qualitative study of adults, guidance, and public adult discussions. The samples are small and do not represent every person with dyscalculia. Dyscalculia can co-occur with other learning differences, so a number tool must not assume a single profile.

Design consequence: support a person to inspect and verify a number-based decision. Do not hide the calculation, silently act on money, or turn performance into a score.

Sources: daily numerical activities study, adult participation study, and adult community discussion.

Possible products to explore

Money and bill checker

Shows totals, change, recurring costs, and assumptions step by step without moving money.

Time and schedule visualiser

Shows duration, departure time, buffers, and what changes when an appointment moves.

Quantity and measurement helper

Supports recipes, sizes, units, and comparisons with a visible worked example.

Pain points and product requirements

1. Time is difficult to estimate and organise

People may know the clock time but struggle to estimate duration, elapsed time, travel buffers, or when to start preparing.

Example: An appointment is at 10:00, but the person cannot tell whether leaving at 9:30 is early enough once travel, parking, and check-in are included.
Possible product: a timeline that separates clock time, duration, preparation, travel, and buffer.
  • show the arithmetic and let the user change assumptions;
  • use words and visual lengths, not colour alone;
  • allow “I am not sure” without forcing a false precision.
Acceptance check: the user can explain why a suggested start time was produced and change or discard it.

2. Money decisions create risk and anxiety

Prices, discounts, change, balances, bills, and recurring payments can require several number operations at once.

Example: A person can afford a purchase in isolation but cannot quickly see how a subscription changes the month’s remaining money.
Possible product: a read-only bill and budget explainer that shows each input, calculation, and uncertainty.
  • never connect directly to a bank by default;
  • make rounding and recurring assumptions explicit;
  • include a second-person review option without sharing all financial data.
Acceptance check: no transaction occurs, and the user can export or delete the calculation inputs.

3. Measurement and quantity are context-dependent

Recipes, sizes, distances, units, and “more or less” can become difficult when the context changes or the numbers are presented in unfamiliar formats.

Example: A recipe uses cups while the available measuring tool uses millilitres, and the person is unsure whether the conversion was applied correctly.
Possible product: a two-column conversion view with physical references and a visible check against the original.
  • preserve the original value and unit;
  • avoid false precision;
  • let the user choose familiar references.
Acceptance check: the user can trace every converted value back to the original and correct it.

4. Shame leads to avoidance or dependence

Repeated mistakes and other people’s impatience can make a person avoid forms, jobs, purchases, or asking for a check.

Example: Someone lets a cashier count change because checking it in public feels too slow and embarrassing.
Possible product: a private, calm checking mode with no streaks, grades, or diagnostic language.
  • use neutral wording;
  • make checking and asking for help normal;
  • never present speed as the goal.
Acceptance check: every result can be reviewed, corrected, skipped, or saved without a failure state.

Evidence boundary and next test

A safe first test is a read-only time or bill explainer with fictional data. It should measure whether people can inspect a calculation and make their own decision. It must not provide financial advice, diagnose dyscalculia, or silently manage money.
