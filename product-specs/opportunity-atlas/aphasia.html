Comparison area · Aphasia

Keep adults with aphasia in the conversation, especially in care

Aphasia is acquired communication loss, often after stroke or brain injury. Word finding, understanding, reading, writing, and conversation access can change over time, so it needs a separate evidence and service pathway from developmental conditions.

Who this evidence represents

The current pass includes people with aphasia, family members, healthcare professionals, and qualitative reviews of service communication. Aphasia is heterogeneous: onset, cause, severity, recovery stage, language, motor access, and fatigue differ. People with aphasia must be involved directly, with accessible research materials and enough time.

Design consequence: treat communication access as a shared responsibility. Do not assume a supporter can speak for the person or that a current difficulty is permanent.

Sources: experience-based co-design study, stroke-care transitions study, qualitative review of local-service barriers, and healthcare communication review.

Possible products to explore

Accessible healthcare conversation

Prepares questions, key words, drawings, yes/no checks, and a reviewable summary.

Personal word and message book

Stores user-chosen names, phrases, topics, and repair strategies without assuming a fixed vocabulary.

Communication-partner guide

Offers short, user-approved guidance to wait, write keywords, confirm meaning, and avoid finishing sentences.

Pain points and product requirements

1. Fast healthcare conversations exclude the person

Hospital and clinic routines can prioritise speed, leaving too little time for comprehension, word finding, repair, or questions.

Example: A clinician explains a discharge plan while the person is tired and cannot produce the words needed to ask what happens next.
Possible product: an accessible visit board with short written keywords, pictures, questions, and a “please wait” signal.
  • support yes/no only when the question is genuinely yes/no;
  • allow the person to correct the record;
  • keep the source and uncertainty visible.
Acceptance check: the person can review, change, or reject a summary before it is shared.

2. Word finding is mistaken for lack of understanding

A person may understand a topic but need a different route to express it.

Example: A person cannot say the name of a medication but can identify it from a picture and explain that it caused a problem.
Possible product: a multimodal message builder with images, keywords, drawing, typing, and personal phrases.
  • allow non-linear message construction;
  • make “I know but cannot say it yet” available;
  • never auto-complete a message without review.
Acceptance check: the user can create an unexpected message and remove any suggested words.

3. Conversation partners can help or block access

Waiting, writing key words, confirming meaning, and reducing distractions can help. Guessing, rushing, or speaking over the person can block participation.

Example: A family member answers a hospital question because the person takes too long, but the answer is wrong.
Possible product: a short user-chosen partner guide with a confirmation step.
  • put the person’s preference before generic advice;
  • make partner guidance optional and context-specific;
  • avoid scoring partner performance.
Acceptance check: the person can remove a partner instruction and decide who can see it.

4. Communication support must survive transitions

Moving from hospital to home, rehabilitation, community services, or work can break continuity of tools, vocabulary, and partner knowledge.

Example: A communication strategy used in rehabilitation is not included in a community appointment, so the person has to explain the same needs again.
Possible product: a portable, user-owned communication profile with selected phrases and recovery notes.
  • export in human-readable formats;
  • make audience and expiry visible;
  • do not create a permanent clinical record by default.
Acceptance check: the user can choose a subset to share, see what was shared, and delete it later.

Evidence boundary and next test

A safe first test is an accessible healthcare conversation-preparation prototype reviewed by people with aphasia and speech-language professionals. It must not claim to restore language, replace therapy, or determine capacity or consent.
