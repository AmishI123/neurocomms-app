Area 2 · Down syndrome

Products that support speech, language, literacy, and participation

The initial evidence points to a gap between what a person understands and how easily other people understand their speech. It also points to the importance of communication partners, AAC, literacy support, and continuity into adult life.

Who this evidence represents

The evidence here includes children, adults, caregivers, teachers, and communication researchers. The first pass has fewer direct self-advocate accounts than caregiver and professional accounts, so claims about adult preferences remain open questions.

What the evidence suggests

A 2024 scoping review found that most Down syndrome speech and language intervention studies involved children and only a few involved adults. A 2025 trial studied AAC language intervention for young children. A small study found variable literacy gains from an AAC feature for adults with Down syndrome.

Design consequence: speech clarity must not be used as a measure of intelligence, understanding, or value. Products must let the person communicate directly and must include adult participation, not only caregiver management.

Sources: communication treatment review, AAC language trial, AAC and adult literacy study, school partner study, and community discussion about speech, signing, AAC, and self-advocacy, and self-advocate research partnership.

Possible products to explore

Person-controlled participation aid

Supports speech, signs, symbols, typing, and other ways to take part in everyday decisions and conversations.

Communication partner guide

Shows the user’s chosen instructions for waiting, listening, offering choices, and checking meaning.

Adult literacy and life-task workspace

Connects reading and writing practice to real tasks such as notices, appointments, work messages, and travel.

Pain points and product requirements

1. Speech may be hard for unfamiliar people to understand

A person may have a lot to say, but a listener may understand only part of it. The listener may then ask a family member or staff member to speak instead.

Example: At a clinic, a person explains where it hurts. The receptionist does not understand and turns to the parent, even though the person is present and can answer.
Possible product: a person-controlled communication and participation aid with text, symbols, photos, typing, signs, or speech output chosen by the user.

Must do:

  • keep the person as the main communicator;
  • support more than yes/no requests, including explanations, questions, disagreement, and private messages;
  • let the person choose whether another person may help.
Acceptance check: a user can create a new message that is not already in a menu, review it, and decide who can hear or see it.

2. Other people may not wait long enough

A person may need more time to understand a question, find words, form speech, or use another communication method.

Example: A teacher asks a question, waits two seconds, and answers it themselves. The student had been preparing an answer.
Possible product: a partner-support tool that displays the user’s chosen instructions: wait, ask one question, offer choices, or check what was heard.

Must do:

  • let the person choose the guidance rather than having staff set it secretly;
  • include a confirmation step: “Did I understand you?”;
  • avoid a score for speed, correct behaviour, or compliance.
Acceptance check: partner guidance can be shown without showing private health information. The person can hide it or change it.

3. Communication support may focus on requests but leave out language growth

A system that only offers food, toilet, activity, or help buttons may not give the person ways to tell stories, explain feelings, ask why, joke, disagree, or learn new words.

Example: A person wants to tell a coworker about something that happened yesterday, but the device has only immediate work requests.
Possible product: a personal language builder that lets the person add words and phrases for stories, interests, relationships, and decisions, with support from a communication professional when appropriate.

Must do:

  • allow new and self-authored messages;
  • keep old vocabulary when the layout changes;
  • let the user own and export the vocabulary.
Acceptance check: adding a new personal phrase does not require the caregiver to replace the existing vocabulary or send data to a vendor.

4. Literacy and communication support may stop after school

School may provide structured teaching, while adult life still requires reading signs, messages, forms, schedules, and workplace information.

Example: An adult can use a supported reading activity but has no practical way to read a work notice or write a question for a healthcare appointment.
Possible product: a participation workspace that connects literacy practice with real tasks: reading a notice, composing a question, checking a message, or following a travel step.

Must do:

  • support adult goals chosen by the person;
  • separate practice feedback from a judgment about ability;
  • offer audio, symbols, text, and partner support as choices.
Acceptance check: the user can use the same content for practice and a real-life task without the app publishing a performance score.

5. Support is spread across family, school, health, and work

People may need several partners to use signs, AAC, literacy support, or communication routines. If each setting does something different, access can disappear during a transition.

Example: A person’s communication book is available at school but not at a job placement. A new staff member does not know how to wait or confirm meaning.
Possible product: a user-owned support profile with a short shareable version and a private full version.

Must do:

  • let the person choose which parts are shared and with whom;
  • show the source and date of each support preference;
  • make a printable or offline version available.
Acceptance check: a new partner can see only the chosen guidance, and the person can revoke access or replace the profile.

Non-goals and open questions

  • Do not treat speech clarity as a measure of intelligence or understanding.
  • Do not design only for parents, teachers, or staff while treating the person as the object of the product.
  • Do not assume AAC, signing, speech, or text is best for every person or every situation.
  • Find more direct adult and self-advocate evidence before selecting an adult product direction.
